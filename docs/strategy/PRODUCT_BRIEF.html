Product Brief · Customer Interrogation

Is Vantage solving a problem people actually have?

We've built a working app that auto-generates “where to shoot” for Atlanta. This brief does the uncomfortable part: pressure-testing the idea from the customer's chair before we build further.

Product Vantage Stage 3 slices built (web preview) Purpose Interrogate, not pitch Date Jul 11, 2026
01

Who this is for, and the job they're hiring us to do

Before features, the customer. A tool only matters at a specific moment, for a specific person.

The job-to-be-done

“When I have time to shoot but don't know where to go — a free evening, a new city, a familiar city gone stale — help me find somewhere worth pointing my camera, timed to good light, without hours of research.”

The traveler

Two days in a city they don't know. High intent, high pain, low local knowledge. Our strongest case.

The stuck local

Shoots their own city, bored of the same spots, wants a nudge. Real, but has strong existing habits.

The small-town shooter

Not in a major metro, genuinely underserved. Our stated differentiator — and our least-tested case.

The weekend hobbyist

Casual, occasional. Lowest intent — may not open an app to decide.

02

The core bet

Everything rests on one hypothesis: photographers regularly face “I want to shoot but don't know where,” and existing tools don't solve it well. If that pain is real and frequent, we have a product. If people mostly return to known spots or decide by instinct, we have a clever demo. The rest of this brief interrogates the beliefs underneath that bet.

03

Interrogating our assumptions

Each is a belief the current build depends on, challenged from the customer's side. The chip is our confidence, not our hope.

POI density equals good street photography
Low confidence
What we assume

That a cluster of markets, plazas, and public art on OpenStreetMap is where you'll get great street shots.

“A dense pile of things isn't a dense pile of people and moments. Truist Park has one giant POI and incredible street energy; a plaza full of monuments can be dead at 2pm.”The gap between map data and photographic reality
The value is strongest in smaller cities — our differentiator
Unvalidated
What we assume

That underserved, non-major cities are where we win — yet we built and validated on Atlanta, a data-rich easy case. Small towns have few OSM POIs, no pro sports, thin events.

“We're claiming the hard case and testing the easy one. The exact place our data gets thin is the exact place we say we're most valuable.”The biggest honesty gap in the plan
People will open an app to decide where to shoot
Behavior risk
What we assume

That there's an open-the-app moment. Today photographers use Instagram location tags, Google Maps, word of mouth, or just walk out the door.

“I already know the three spots I like. Why would I check an app?” — vs — “I'm in Lisbon for 48 hours and I'd kill for exactly this.”Two customers, opposite verdicts — segment decides everything
A photography app can launch with no photographs
Trust risk
What we assume

That tonal-gradient cards are an acceptable placeholder. But the mockup made example-shot galleries central — photographers decide with their eyes.

“Show me what people actually shot here and I'm sold in two seconds. Tell me in text and I'm skeptical. A photo app with no photos feels unfinished.”Imagery may be core value, not polish
Light timing matters as much for street as for landscape
Borrowed signal
What we assume

We inherited golden-hour from the landscape mockup. But street/portrait is often about the person and the moment — harsh midday light and overcast are prime for street.

“Golden hour is a landscape obsession. Half my best street frames are flat noon light with a great subject. Don't down-rank my afternoon.”Are we over-indexing on a landscape metric?
Events (World Cup, Braves) are a useful signal — worth showing
Plausible
What we assume

That surfacing games and festivals adds value. It probably does — crowds are street gold — but some are obvious (locals know the Braves are home) and it can read as noise.

“The World Cup being in town, I know. What I didn't know is the farmers market three blocks away at 8am. Surface the non-obvious.”Events help — if they tell me something I didn't know
04

The moment of use we haven't nailed

There are two very different customers hiding in one app, and they want opposite things:

“Right now” mode — I'm out, camera in hand, tell me where to walk in the next hour. Wants: near-me, instant, live light, minimal reading.
“Planning” mode — I'm at home plotting tomorrow or an upcoming trip. Wants: browse, save, build a route, richer detail.

Today's app leans “planning” (a ranked city list) but is framed “right now” (“Today in Atlanta,” a golden-hour countdown). Picking the primary moment — and designing hard for it — matters more than any single feature.

06

The map, from the customer's side

The proposed map re-plots the homepage districts as score teardrops. From the customer's chair, one question decides its worth: does it help me decide where to physically go?

If pins sit at true relative locations, the map answers what the list can't — proximity, clustering, “what's around me.” If pins are placed decoratively (as the comp shows), the customer will still read position as location, and we'll have quietly taught them something false. For a scouting app, a map that misrepresents where things are is worse than no map.

“A dot on a map is a promise about where to walk. If it's just decoration, I'll trust it, get it wrong, and blame the app.”Truthful geography isn't a nice-to-have here
07

Cheap ways to find out if we're right

Before building more, buy down the riskiest assumptions for near-zero cost.

  • The “would you go?” test. Show 5–10 street photographers the current Atlanta list. Do the top areas match where they'd actually shoot? Directly tests the POI-density assumption.
  • Run the small-city case. Point the existing script at a mid-size, non-metro city. See how thin the data gets. Tests our stated differentiator honestly.
  • The travel-intent probe. Ask photographers: “new city, 2 days — would you use this?” vs “your home city — would you?” Find which segment has real pull.
  • Photos vs no-photos. Show the same area with and without example shots. Measure trust/intent. Decides whether imagery is core or polish.
  • Light relevance. Ask street shooters how much golden hour actually drives their where. Confirms or kills a borrowed landscape metric.
08

Honest verdict

A promising engine, an unproven promise.

We've shown we can auto-generate a plausible “where to shoot” list from free data — the technical bet is largely de-risked. What's not yet tested is whether it solves a real, frequent customer problem better than free habits — especially in the small cities we claim as our edge.

Strongest

The traveler in an unfamiliar city. Clear job, weak alternatives, high pain.

Riskiest

“POI density = good street,” and validating on easy Atlanta while claiming hard small towns.

Do next

Talk to real photographers before more features. One “would you go?” session beats a slice.
